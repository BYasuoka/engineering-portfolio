Early Design Competition

RC Battle Boat

This 15-week design competition challenged teams to build a remote-controlled battle boat that could survive head-to-head freshwater rounds. The project emphasizes iterative prototyping, waterproofing, propulsion tradeoffs, and the discipline to prioritize what would actually work on competition day.

Team

Brenton's Buccaneers

Report authors listed: Matthew Ashworth, Bella Kaze, Zach Sheehan, and Brenton Yasuoka.

Outcome

Mobility and survivability came first

The report repeatedly shows that the team chose an unsinkable, controllable boat over a riskier weapon-heavy design, which is a very real engineering tradeoff under schedule pressure.

Design Priorities

Waterproofing, control, and stability over showier features

Hull and buoyancy

The team grounded early design decisions in buoyancy and stability, aiming for enough water-contact area to float well without creating an unstable platform.

Remote control

Steering ideas were informed by toy RC systems and servo-actuated linkages, which helped the team think in practical, accessible mechanisms rather than abstract control theory.

Motor choice

The report compares brushed and brushless motors and leans on the simplicity and water tolerance of brushed motors for an aquatic student project.

Battery and packaging

Battery selection, waterproof enclosures, and internal layout were treated as system decisions because mass, safety, and runtime all feed back into the boat's behavior.

Iteration Story

  • An outboard motor concept reduced how much of the hull needed to be waterproofed.
  • An internal dual-propeller concept created harder sealing problems because spinning shafts had to pass through the hull.
  • Testing pushed the team toward two stationary outboard DC motors mounted to the base as the most workable propulsion path.
  • The report openly acknowledges where more time for testing would have improved both movement and system refinement.

Engineering Judgment

  • The team explored weapon concepts, but the report shows that safety, waterproofing, and schedule risk forced repeated reassessment.
  • One of the most useful lessons is that a technically ambitious subsystem is not automatically worth keeping if it threatens the rest of the build.
  • The writeup treats ethical decision-making as part of the engineering work, especially when testing and competition safety are involved.

What This Project Shows

Early-stage design education done the right way

Rapid prototyping mindset

The report repeatedly frames success around building, testing, learning, and adjusting rather than expecting the first idea to be correct.

Systems thinking

Waterproofing, propulsion, battery choice, hull geometry, and control strategy are all presented as interdependent choices rather than separate assignments.

Honest post-project reflection

The team is clear about where schedule, testing, and integration fell short, which makes the report more credible and more valuable as a learning artifact.

Competition-ready prioritization

A controllable boat that survives the round is often a better engineering result than a flashier concept that cannot be validated in time.

Project Takeaways

  • Waterproofing is rarely a detail in marine projects; it becomes a design driver very quickly.
  • Choosing a simpler mechanism can create more overall performance once sealing and maintenance are considered.
  • Testing cadence matters as much as concept creativity in team design competitions.
  • Good engineering judgment sometimes means narrowing scope, not expanding it.
